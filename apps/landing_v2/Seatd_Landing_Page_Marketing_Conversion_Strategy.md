# Seatd Landing Page — Marketing & Conversion Strategy

**Document purpose:** Define how the Seatd marketing landing page should be structured, positioned, written, and visually directed to persuade restaurant owners and operators to request a demo or start a sales conversation.

**Audience:** Seatd product/design/development team, marketers, sales, and anyone responsible for building the marketing site.

**Primary conversion goal:** Get qualified restaurant owners/operators to **request a demo / book a walkthrough**.

**Secondary conversion goal:** Let interested but not-yet-ready buyers understand Seatd well enough to remember it, share it internally, or continue exploring.

**This is a strategy/specification document, not a visual design.**

---

# 1. What the Landing Page Must Accomplish

The landing page should not behave like a normal software homepage that lists features.

It has one job:

> Make a restaurant owner believe that Seatd can make their floor easier to run and worth paying for.

The visitor should understand the following within roughly the first 20–30 seconds:

1. **What Seatd is**
2. **What problem it solves**
3. **Why the problem matters financially or operationally**
4. **How Seatd works**
5. **Why it is different from a POS or reservation system**
6. **Why the owner should trust it**
7. **What they should do next**

The page should move the buyer through this mental sequence:

```text
I recognise this problem
        ↓
This looks useful
        ↓
I understand how it works
        ↓
This would fit my restaurant
        ↓
This looks simple to introduce
        ↓
I can see the business value
        ↓
I trust the product/company
        ↓
I want to see it in action
```

That journey matters more than having a visually impressive page.

---

# 2. Core Marketing Position

Seatd should be positioned around the physical restaurant floor.

It should **not** present itself as:

- another POS;
- a reservations platform;
- a digital menu;
- an ordering system;
- a loyalty app;
- a generic restaurant-management suite.

Seatd's territory is:

> **Restaurant floor operations.**

The recommended product story is:

# See → Respond → Improve

### See

Know what is happening across the restaurant floor in real time.

### Respond

Give staff and guests a faster way to communicate when attention is needed.

### Improve

Use operational information to understand and improve how the floor performs.

> **Important product-claim rule:** If the proposed analytics/Intelligence features have not shipped when the page launches, do not present them as current functionality. Either omit the "Improve" product claims, describe them as "coming soon", or replace them with benefits supported by the live product.

---

# 3. The Main Message to Restaurant Owners

Restaurant owners do not primarily buy software.

They buy:

- smoother operations;
- fewer service problems;
- better guest experiences;
- more productive staff;
- better use of available seating;
- more visibility into the business;
- less chaos during busy service periods.

The landing page therefore should **translate every feature into an operational outcome**.

## Weak messaging

> Real-time table management.

## Better messaging

> See which tables are available, occupied, or need attention without walking the entire floor.

---

## Weak messaging

> QR guest assistance.

## Better messaging

> Let guests request help from their table instead of trying to catch a waiter's attention.

---

## Weak messaging

> Live dashboard.

## Better messaging

> Give your front-of-house team one shared view of what's happening across the floor.

---

## The rule

For every feature shown, answer:

> **"So what?"**

If the page cannot answer that, the feature should not receive much marketing space.

---

# 4. Ideal Customer the Page Should Speak To

The page should not be written for "all restaurants."

The initial messaging should feel especially relevant to:

- high-volume casual restaurants;
- pubs and sports bars;
- restaurants with 25+ tables;
- restaurants with multiple floors or service areas;
- venues with patios, decks, bars, smoking sections, lounges, or outdoor seating;
- restaurants where staff frequently move between sections;
- hotel restaurants;
- restaurant groups.

The visitor should think:

> "This looks like it was built for a restaurant like mine."

---

# 5. Recommended Conversion Strategy

The primary CTA should be:

> **Book a Demo**

or:

> **See Seatd in Action**

"Buy Now" is too aggressive for a restaurant operations product that may require setup, onboarding, floor configuration, and internal buy-in.

"Learn More" is too weak for the primary action.

The page should use **one main conversion action repeatedly** instead of changing the CTA every section.

Recommended hierarchy:

### Primary CTA

**Book a Demo**

### Secondary CTA

**See How It Works**

The secondary CTA should scroll the visitor into the product story rather than sending them away from the page.

---

# 6. CTA Placement

The primary CTA should appear at least in:

1. Hero
2. After the first product explanation
3. After proof / benefits
4. Final conversion section
5. Sticky or persistent navigation on desktop, if appropriate

Do not add a CTA after every paragraph.

The page should persuade between asks.

      ↓

      ↓

      ↓

      ↓

The exact number of visual sections can change during implementation, but the **persuasion order** should remain close to this.

# 8. Section 1 — Hero

The hero must answer three questions instantly:

> What is this?

> Why should I care?

> What should I do next?

Avoid clever wording that makes the visitor think.

## Recommended message direction

### Eyebrow

Restaurant floor operations

### Headline direction

> **Know what's happening on your floor.**

Alternative directions:

> **A smarter way to run your restaurant floor.**

> **Your restaurant floor. Live.**

> **See your floor. Respond faster. Run a better service.**

The first option is the strongest starting point because it is simple, memorable, and owns the product category.

### Supporting copy direction

Explain Seatd in one sentence:

> Seatd gives your team a live view of table activity, lets guests request assistance directly from their table, and helps you run service with less guesswork.

Do not use more than approximately two short lines of supporting copy on desktop.

# 9. Hero Visual Strategy

The hero visual is extremely important because Seatd is a visual product.

Do **not** use:

- abstract 3D shapes;
- floating gradient blobs;
- random restaurant stock photography as the main visual;
- generic dashboard charts;
- illustrations that do not show the actual product.

The best hero visual should make the product understandable without reading.

## Recommended visual concept

Show a **realistic Seatd restaurant floor interface** in the center of the hero.

The visitor should immediately see:

- floor plan;
- multiple tables;
- available tables;
- occupied tables;
- a table requesting attention;
- subtle live-status activity.

Around or behind the primary floor interface, optionally show small contextual product surfaces:

```text
        Guest request
       "Bill please"
             │
             ▼

Waiter app ── FLOOR MAP ── Entrance display

             ▲
             │
       Live restaurant
```

The product itself should be the visual hero.

# 10. Hero Motion

Motion can make Seatd feel alive because realtime activity is one of the core product benefits.

Use motion to demonstrate function rather than decorate the page.

Possible hero sequence:

1. Table changes from available → occupied
2. Guest request appears on another table
3. Attention indicator activates
4. Waiter resolves the request
5. Status updates across the interface

The entire sequence should make the message obvious:

> Everything stays in sync.

Avoid excessive animation.

The experience should feel:

- controlled;
- premium;
- responsive;
- operational;
- calm.

Not:

- playful;
- gamified;
- futuristic for the sake of being futuristic.

# 11. Section 2 — Immediate Credibility

Before asking the visitor to invest attention in the rest of the page, reduce perceived risk.

Eventually this area should show:

- customer logos;
- number of active venues;
- tables managed;
- guest requests handled;
- service response improvements;
- testimonials;
- pilot results.

Example future proof bar:

```text
12 venues   •   840+ tables   •   18,000 guest requests handled
```

Do not fabricate numbers.

Until Seatd has meaningful customer proof, replace fake social proof with honest trust signals such as:

- "Built for busy hospitality teams"
- "No guest app required"
- "Works alongside your existing restaurant systems"
- "Live floor updates"
- "Simple table QR experience"

Once real customer results exist, replace generic trust statements with numbers.

**Numbers beat adjectives.**

# 12. Section 3 — Sell the Problem Before the Product

Owners should recognise their restaurant before Seatd starts explaining screens.

A visual problem section could communicate:

> Busy service shouldn't depend on everyone remembering everything.

Then surface common problems.

### Problem 1

Staff do not always know which tables are actually available.

### Problem 2

Guests struggle to get someone's attention during busy periods.

### Problem 3

Different sections of the restaurant become disconnected.

### Problem 4

Managers see the floor, but rarely have useful information about how it performs over time.

Keep this section short.

The goal is recognition, not fear.

# 16. Section 7 — "How It Works"

Keep setup simple.

The page should communicate that Seatd does **not** require replacing the restaurant's technology stack.

Recommended flow:

### 01 — Map your restaurant

Create floors and tables that match the physical venue.

### 02 — Give your team access

Staff use the live floor during service.

### 03 — Add table QR codes

Guests can request help without installing anything.

### 04 — Run service

Table activity and guest requests stay visible in one place.

### 05 — Learn from the floor

When Intelligence/analytics is available, historical activity becomes operational insight.

The message underneath should be:

> **Seatd works alongside the systems you already use.**

This addresses one of the biggest likely objections before it is asked.

# 23. Analytics / Intelligence Section

When implemented, analytics could become one of Seatd's strongest owner-facing sales arguments.

Do not show a generic SaaS analytics dashboard full of meaningless charts.

Each visual should answer a real operational question.

Examples:

### Which tables work hardest?

Table utilisation heatmap.

### When does service pressure peak?

Occupancy + assistance requests across the day.

The analytics interface should feel like a **restaurant operations report**, not a business-intelligence tool.

# 34. FAQ — Objections the Page Should Answer

The FAQ should be a sales tool, not miscellaneous documentation.

Questions should include:

## Does Seatd replace my POS?

No. Seatd is focused on live restaurant-floor operations and is designed to work alongside your existing systems.

## Do guests need to download an app?

No. Guests access table assistance through the browser by scanning the table QR code.

## What happens if the internet connection drops?

Explain the actual supported offline behaviour accurately based on the deployed clients.

## Can we use our existing floor layout?

Explain the owner floor configuration process.

## Can I have multiple floors or seating areas?

Yes, based on Seatd's current floor model.

## Can staff see guest requests?

Yes.

## Can I customise the guest request buttons?

Yes, using configured guest actions.

## How long does setup take?

Only give a concrete answer after onboarding has been tested enough to support it.

## Does this handle reservations?

No. Seatd intentionally focuses on live floor operations rather than reservations.

## Does it process payments?

No.

This honesty helps sharpen the product category.

# 39. Typography Direction

Typography should feel editorial and premium.

Use:

- strong headline scale;
- tight headline line-height;
- generous whitespace;
- restrained body width;
- short paragraphs;
- clear hierarchy.

Avoid:

- tiny SaaS text;
- excessive bolding;
- headings that are all the same size;
- long center-aligned paragraphs;
- writing every sentence as marketing hype.

Owners should be able to skim the page quickly.

# 44. Mobile Landing Page

Even if restaurant owners eventually evaluate the product on desktop, many will first open the marketing link from:

- WhatsApp;
- email;
- social media;
- their phone.

The mobile page must not be an afterthought.

Prioritise:

- instant headline clarity;
- visible CTA;
- readable product screenshots;
- short sections;
- large tap targets;
- fast load times;
- videos that do not block the page.

# 45. Page Performance

A beautiful landing page that loads slowly will hurt marketing.

Particularly avoid:

- huge autoplay hero videos;
- oversized Three.js experiences;
- multiple WebGL scenes;
- uncompressed restaurant photography;
- dozens of animation libraries;
- loading the entire marketing experience before showing content.

If 3D or advanced motion is used, it should earn its performance cost.

Seatd's product UI is already visually interesting enough to carry the page.

---

# 46. Trust & Security Section

Restaurant owners may eventually ask what happens to their data.

Keep the homepage treatment short.

Potential trust points supported by the current architecture:

- guests do not need accounts;
- guest assistance is table-scoped;
- no guest PII is required for the basic guest assist flow;
- restaurant operations are tenant-scoped;
- display access uses revocable device tokens.

Do not make broad compliance or security certifications unless Seatd has actually achieved them.

A dedicated security/privacy page can grow later.

# 65. Source-of-Truth / Product-Claim Guardrail

The current Seatd system supports the core marketing story around:

- multi-tenant restaurant floor layouts;
- table availability / occupancy / attention visibility;
- owner floor configuration;
- waiter floor operations;
- entrance display;
- table QR guest assistance;
- configurable guest actions;
- realtime staff/display updates.

The following are recommended future directions and should **not** be marketed as shipped until implemented:

- floor-performance analytics;
- utilisation reporting;
- turnover reporting;
- historical response analytics;
- restaurant-group performance comparison;
- individual waiter performance;
- POS-triggered automatic occupancy;
- ROI reporting.

This distinction is important.

A strong landing page does not need to exaggerate Seatd.

The current product already has a clear visual and operational story. The job of the marketing page is to make that story **immediately understandable, commercially relevant, and trustworthy**.
